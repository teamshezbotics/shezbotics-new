One skill, taught first

Most bad answers come from vague instructions, not bad tools. In the first session every team learns the same four-part instruction, then uses it on their own work.

  1. Role

    Who should it be? “You are an experienced accounts assistant.”

  2. Context

    What does it need to know? “We supply raw materials to industrial buyers.”

  3. Task

    What exactly do you want? “Draft a reply to this enquiry about a delivery delay.”

  4. Format

    How should it come back? “Short email. Formal. Under 120 words.”

A vague instruction gets a vague answer, from AI and from people.

Honest about the limits

Teams that know the edges use these tools better, so the first session starts with what they can't do.

Good at

  • Drafting and rewriting
  • Summarising long documents
  • Explaining and formatting
  • Structuring messy information
  • Getting a first version started

Not reliable for

  • Facts, figures and dates
  • Anything legal or statutory
  • Your company's own data
  • Final judgement
  • Being sure when it is wrong

“It never says ‘I am not sure.’ That is the part you have to supply.”

What stays out of AI

  • Directors' personal or financial details
  • GST, TDS and statutory credentials
  • Employee personal data
  • Donor and trust records
  • Customer contracts and unpublished pricing
  • Anything you would not email outside the company

Everyone receives this list in writing on day one. Check every figure before it leaves your desk: the judgement and the accountability stay with you, not the tool.
